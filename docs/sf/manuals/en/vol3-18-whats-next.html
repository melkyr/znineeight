What You Have Learned, What Is Next

This is the last chapter of Volume III, and like the last chapter of Volume II it introduces nothing. Every page before it taught one part of working in the era; this page gathers the parts, says plainly what you can now do, and points at where the rest of the manual goes from here. It carries no program and no figure, because there is no new compiler behavior left to demonstrate. The volume is a closed set: if a sentence here claims something the chapters did not teach, the sentence is wrong and the chapter is right.

The exit state printed on the title page is that you can ship software for 1998 hardware. The chapters measured that as they went. The programs in docs/sf/manuals/src/vol3/ were compiled with the compiler rebuilt from the committed seed, run, and captured, and the Win9x-only ones were cross-built and run under wine; the transcripts on the pages are those captured outputs. So the summary below is a summary of things this volume has already shown working, not a promise about behavior this page adds.

The compiler under test is the seed build whose two hops closed at 8216fedc…, the recorded fixed point for seed v89. The recipe is the one the title page gives: the committed seed compiles the current source, and the result compiles it again to the same binary. That the two builds agree is the gate that the compiler is self-hosting and deterministic, and it is what lets every transcript in this volume be tied to one exact compiler state rather than to "whatever was installed."

Honest note. Finishing this volume means you can ship for 1998; it does not mean the whole era was proved here, and the difference matters. The Win9x claims were verified the only way this development host allows: the emitted C89 cross-compiled with i686-w64-mingw32-gcc and run under wine with line endings normalized. The OpenWatcom build_owc.bat/wcc386 path is emitted, not run, and the real Windows 98 screenshots remain pending figures, not claims. Some of this volume is also opinion, and says so where it is. Read it as a discipline you can apply on the machine you have, and read the Reference for lookup — not as a transcript of every machine the era shipped.

What you can ship for 1998

The short answer is a program, built from Z98, that runs on the machine the era actually had. That breaks into the concrete things the second half of the volume delivered. Each row is something a chapter showed you how to do and, where the target allowed, ran.

That is the exit state, and it is worth stating what it is not. It is not "I can write Z98" — Volume II gave you that, and this volume assumed it on every page. It is not "I can write C89", either; you can, because chapter 3 exists, but the language you ship in is still Z98. What this volume adds is the target. You can now point Z98 at a Pentium II with 32 MB and hand someone something that runs there, and you can say without hedging which parts of that job Z98 is good for and where C89, C++98, or assembly does it better.

The volume in one argument

Under the practical list is a single argument, and the volume made it in order. It is worth restating, because a list can read as a feature count when the point is a discipline.

Chapter 1 fixed the machine: memory, not CPU, is the binding constraint, and every later decision follows from that. Chapter 2 set the register with the full inventory of what Z98 gives and what it withholds, each absence paired with the era alternative that takes its place. Chapters 3, 4, and 5 said plainly when another language wins, and the through-line is not "Z98 is always right" but the load-bearing fact that Z98 emits C89, so the two mix freely. Chapter 6 named what the machine cannot do, and chapter 7 named the friction you will hit — each item ending with what to do about it, not with a complaint.

Then the volume split in two. Chapters 9 and 10 carried the mental shift: suspension is a language statement, a function with @asyncSuspend is compiled to a step machine, and std.async owns the scheduler and the per-task frame pool, so the state word and re-entry switch you would have hand-rolled are written for you. Chapters 8 and 11–17 were the practical half: build, interoperate, talk to the network, drive the debug API, shape DirectX, debug, budget memory, and package. The argument closes where it started — the era had limits, and respecting them is the point.

None of that is a claim that the era is pleasant. It is a claim that the era is knowable, and that knowing it is the work the blueprint put in this volume rather than in the language. The language chapters taught you to write Z98; these taught you to ship it to a machine with a fixed memory ceiling, a C89 toolchain, and no network to fall back on.

Reference and HOWTO are for lookup

There is a second way to use this manual, and it is not reading front to back. A shipped program will send you to a page for one fact: the exact argument order of std.net.bind, what @intCast does with a negative value, the name of the OpenWatcom flag that turns warnings into errors, whether defer runs on the return path. For that you want the Reference and, when it ships, the HOWTO — not a chapter you read whole.

The shipped Reference pages today are Volume IV — Reference, Volume IV — Builtins, and Volume IV — HTML style. They are lookup material: the builtins page catalogues the runtime surface behind the introspection and conversion builtins this volume's programs use, and the style page is the contract the pages you are reading were written against. The rest of the Reference — the grammar, types, pointers, aggregates, errors, optionals, memory, the standard library, the PAL, diagnostics, and the command line — is (planned).

The HOWTO, Volume V — HOWTO, is entirely (planned), and its subject is the other half of lookup: not "what does this word mean" but "how do I do this one task." Its table of contents already names the recipes this volume implies — rebuild the compiler from seed, target Win9x, use the arena correctly, write and drive a coroutine, sockets under Z98, C interop, link against C89 libraries, debug generated C, read the diagnostics, pack for diskette. Until those pages exist, the chapters here and the example programs are the authority, and the Builtins page is the one lookup page that ships today.

The examples tree has full case studies

The manual ships its programs, not just fragments. Every chapter that teaches code names a complete file under docs/sf/manuals/src/, and the file is the case study for that chapter: you can open it, read it whole, compile it with the seed-built compiler, and compare your output to the transcript on the page. Volume II's programs are the language's case studies, and Volume III adds the era's. The Volume III set is:

  • docs/sf/manuals/src/vol3/hello.z98 — the Win9x build walkthrough, from source to .exe (chapter 8).
  • docs/sf/manuals/src/vol3/tasks.z98 — coroutines driven by std.async, with the frame size and the round-robin step order (chapter 9).
  • docs/sf/manuals/src/vol3/echo.z98 — a line echo server built from coroutines, one task per connection (chapter 10).
  • docs/sf/manuals/src/vol3/getenv.z98 — calling C through extern fn and reading the mangled emitted names (chapter 11).
  • docs/sf/manuals/src/vol3/http-mini.z98 — a WinSock server that serves one HTTP/1.0 request over the std.net surface (chapter 12).
  • docs/sf/manuals/src/vol3/dbg-mini.z98 — launching a target under the Win32 debug API and planting an INT3 breakpoint (chapter 13).

Chapters 14 through 17 ship no program, and the pages say why in each case: chapter 14's DirectX example was dropped by ruling, and chapters 15 through 17 teach a workflow, a budget, and a release shape whose evidence is the tool output and the emitted tree rather than a new program. The two programs behind the language's own working habits are Volume II's arena.z98 for the memory half and shapes2.z98 for the object half; if you arrived here fluent in Z98, those are the files to reread when an era program gets large.

Running the set is deliberate work, not trivia. It is how you confirm that the compiler on your machine behaves like the one these pages were written against; if a transcript and your run disagree, the debugger workflow is how you find out which side is wrong.

The handoff: Reference, HOWTO, Internals

The volumes after this one are three, and only the first has pages today. They are not three more languages; they are three different uses of the one you already know.

  • Volume IV — Reference. The lookup set for the language. Its title page and builtins page ship today, and the rest is (planned). When you know the question — one builtin's behavior, one grammar rule, one command-line flag — this is where the answer belongs, not in a chapter you have to read to find the sentence.
  • Volume V — HOWTO (planned). Recipes for the specific tasks a Z98 program has to do. The volume you just finished is written once and read as an argument; the HOWTO is written to be opened, run, and closed.
  • Volume VI — Internals (planned). The compiler itself, for its maintainers: the allocator, the phases, the emitter, the async frame layout, the build. It is the volume the seed model points at — everything this manual taught you about determinism and the fixed point is implemented there.

The path through them depends on why you picked up the manual. If you set out to ship software for 1998, you have what you came for, and the Reference is the one to keep open beside the editor. If you set out to understand Z98 itself, the Internals volume is the next stop, and the Reference is its companion. The volume index on the Contents page is the map of all six.

Where to go next

The chapter before this one is the volume's other summary of practice: chapter 17, Packaging and shipping, which is the last thing you do before someone else runs your program. If you are looking for a specific part of the volume, the Contents page lists all nineteen chapters, and each chapter links the others it depends on rather than repeating them.

For lookup rather than reading, start with Volume IV — Reference and, for the runtime calls behind the builtins, Volume IV — Builtins. If you arrived at this volume without the language underneath it, the on-ramp is Volume II — Learning Z98, and its first chapter is the comparison this volume's honesty chapters extend to the era.

The shortest path back into the material is the list of programs above. Each one is a complete file that compiles with the seed-built compiler and runs, and the chapter that names it has the transcript and the explanation. That is the loop this manual was built for: read the argument once, then keep the Reference and the examples open while you ship. For now, the contents page shows what is planned and what has shipped, and the shipped Reference and example pages are the ones you can rely on today.
