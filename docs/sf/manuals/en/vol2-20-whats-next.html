What You Have Learned, What Is Next

This is the last chapter of the volume, and it introduces nothing. Every chapter before it taught one part of Z98; this page gathers the parts, says plainly what you can now do, and points at the volume that comes after. It carries no program and no figure, because there is no new compiler behavior left to demonstrate. The volume is a closed set: if a sentence here claims something the chapters did not teach, it is a mistake on this page and not a feature of the language.

The exit state printed on the title page is that you can write Z98 fluently, in its own syntax and its own idioms. The chapters measured that as they went. Every program in docs/sf/manuals/src/vol2/ was compiled with the compiler rebuilt from the committed seed, run, and captured, and the transcripts on the pages are those captured outputs. The summary below is therefore a summary of things the volume has already shown working, not a promise about behavior this page adds.

Honest note. Finishing this volume makes you fluent in Z98; it does not make you ready to ship for 1998. Those are two different skills, and the blueprint keeps them in two different volumes. This volume taught the language and the compiler's behavior on the host machine. It did not teach how to fit a program into 32 MB, which C89 dialect OpenWatcom accepts, how to drive WinSock, or what the era's other tools do better. It also did not remove the language's own limits: there are still no generics, no templates, no classes, no exceptions, and no threads, and there is no allocator or destructor running behind your back. Chapter 1 set out those limits deliberately, and chapter 16 measured what replaces them. Volume III is where the machine and the honesty chapters live. Read this page as "I can write Z98", not as "I can ship on a Pentium II."

What you can now do

The language surface is complete. Each area below has a chapter behind it, and the chapter is where the measured behavior lives if you need to check a detail.

Types and pointers

You can name every primitive and every integer width. Chapter 2 covered the primitives, arbitrary widths u1 through u64, isize and usize, and bool, void, noreturn, and c_char; it fixed the width rule, that @sizeOf reports the carrier while @bitSizeOf reports the declared width; and it taught the current conversion behavior, that integer-to-integer conversions are implicit today with warning[3000] at declarations and assignments and silent at call sites, with @intCast as the explicit, warning-free form.

You can work with every pointer form. Chapter 3 covered *T, *const T, *volatile T, [*]T, and **T; address-of with & and dereference with .*; indexing with ptr[i] only on a many-item pointer; field access through ptr.field with its automatic dereference; the rule that const is frontend-only while volatile is preserved in the emitted C; and the cast and conversion builtins the volume catalogued in chapter 15.

Aggregates

You can build every aggregate the language has, and inspect its layout when you need to. Chapter 4: plain and nested structs, field order, and @offsetOf, @sizeOf, and @alignOf. Chapter 5: packed struct, LSB-first packing, the 31-bit field cap, and @bitOffsetOf and @bitSizeOf, for the two cases that earn them, hardware registers and wire formats. Chapter 6: enum and enum(uN), the enum conversion builtins, and enum ranges in switch. Chapter 7: bare, packed, and tagged unions, naked tags, and anonymous struct payloads, and when each form is right. Chapter 8: tuples, why they exist (printing and grouped returns), and why not to use them for anything else. Chapter 9: fixed arrays, slices, []const T, .ptr and .len, slicing both forms, the array-to-slice coercion, and const propagation.

Control flow and cleanup

You can write the statements and the expressions. Chapter 10 covered if and if expressions, optional capture with if (x) |v|, while with a continue expression, while (opt) |v|, for with index capture, switch with inclusive and exclusive ranges and its mandatory else prong, labeled loops, and value-less labeled blocks. Chapter 11 added defer and errdefer: last-in-first-out ordering, execution on every path, and the rule that break, continue, return, and try are rejected inside a cleanup body.

Failure, absence, and memory

This is the half of the language the volume calls its mental shifts. Chapter 12 is the first: error sets and error unions, error{Foo, Bar}, !T and E!T, try, catch |err|, errdefer, and error.Tag; it explained why this replaces -1 returns, why orelse is rejected on an error union, and why there is no anyerror. Chapter 13 is the companion: ?T, null, orelse, if (opt) |v|, while (opt) |v|, and why ?*T keeps the same representation. Chapter 14 is the second shift: why there is no malloc and no free, the std.arena API, the ArenaError![*]u8 result that is unwrapped with try or catch and never with orelse, and the dual-arena pattern for two lifetimes.

Builtins, the library, and output

Chapter 15 catalogued every builtin the language has, grouped by use, with an example for each, and fixed the rule that an unknown builtin is error[3000]. Chapter 16 explained the absences that shape the rest: no methods, no generics, no comptime beyond the builtins, and the two patterns that replace them, the init function and the manual vtable. Chapter 17 named the thirteen modules the std namespace actually exports and gave the smallest call that proves each one is reachable. Chapter 18 took apart the one variadic call, print, with its five specifiers, its tuple-literal argument form, and the diagnostics that fire when a format and its argument disagree.

The habits you carry

Chapter 19 collected the working habits the earlier chapters had been demonstrating all along. They are not features; they are what keeps a Z98 program a Z98 program rather than a C program that happens to compile under Z98. In the order the chapter lists them:

  • Arena ownership — the buffer is yours and the arena is a cursor over it; allocation is fallible and reclamation is one reset. (Chapter 14.)
  • Init functions — an object becomes usable through a free function that fills its fields and returns it, because there are no constructors. (Chapter 14, chapter 16.)
  • Manual virtual tables — a struct of function pointers plus one erased data pointer, because there are no methods and no classes. (Chapter 16.)
  • Dual arenas — one arena for data that lives for the whole run, one for data that dies every phase. (Chapter 14.)
  • Runtime initialization — complex global state is built by a dedicated init() called at startup, not by a large constant initializer.
  • undefined is not zero — an uninitialized value holds the poison fill under the default safe build and nothing at all under -ffast; it is never quietly zeroed. (Chapter 14, chapter 4.)

The two working programs behind those habits are docs/sf/manuals/src/vol2/arena.z98 for the memory half and docs/sf/manuals/src/vol2/shapes2.z98 for the object half. Between them they exercise every idiom on the list, and chapter 19 walks through the sequence they share.

What the volume did not give you

The honesty in this volume is not an afterthought. Chapter 1 made the comparison explicit and framed Z98 as a higher-level C89, not a smaller modern Zig, and the chapters that followed kept the framing honest. So the summary would be false if it stopped at what you can do. Here is the other column, stated as plainly as the chapters stated it.

There are no generics and no templates; a container is a concrete struct with a field per element type, the way ShapeList is in chapter 16. There are no classes and no method syntax; a call is a free function and the receiver is an ordinary parameter. There are no exceptions; failure travels in the return type as an error union. There are no threads and no preemption, and no dynamic dispatch beyond the manual vtable you build yourself. There is no anyerror, and comptime does not exist beyond the specific builtins chapter 15 catalogued. There is no constructor and no destructor, and no operation allocates behind your back; when a program needs storage it declares a buffer and hands an arena over it. The standard library is thirteen modules and, in the volume's own words, a set of concrete hand-written helpers rather than a convenience layer. The printer takes five specifiers and no width, precision, or alignment.

None of that is a defect report. Each item is the trade the language made for deterministic C89 emission and no hidden control flow. What you get in return is that every call is a plain function you can read, the generated C has no machinery you did not write, and a printed value is always the value.

The handoff: Volume III, Working in the Era

The volume after this one is Volume III — Working in the Era. Its first chapter now ships: chapter 2, Honesty: what Z98 does and does not give you; the rest of the volume is (planned). Its entry state is the exit state of this volume, someone fluent in Z98, and its exit state is someone who can ship software for 1998 hardware. Where this volume taught the language, Volume III is about the discipline, and the blueprint says it is heavier on prose than code because the era has more opinions than the language does.

The material it adds is the material this volume deliberately left out. It opens on the 1998 machine: a Pentium II, 32 MB of memory, Windows 95 or 98, IDE drives, and a 640×480 screen, and the argument that memory, not CPU, is the binding constraint. Then come the six honesty chapters, which are the spine of the volume: what Z98 does and does not give you, and when C89, C++98, or assembly is the better choice; what you cannot do on a Pentium II; and the friction you will actually hit. From there it turns practical: building for Win9x with OpenWatcom, talking to C, WinSock, the Win32 Debug API, DirectX under CINTERFACE, the debugger workflow, memory budgets, and packaging and shipping.

Volume III is also where the third mental shift lands. This volume had two, error unions in chapter 12 and the arena in chapter 14; the blueprint's third is the coroutines chapter, III.9, where the suspending-function analysis and the std.async scheduler replace the state machine you would otherwise hand-roll. The builtins for that shift are already catalogued in chapter 15, and the module that drives them is in chapter 17; Volume III is where they are put to work.

What carries over unchanged is the working half of this volume: the error unions and the defer and errdefer cleanup, the arena and the dual-arena pattern, and the init function and manual vtable. What changes is the target. The same language, pointed at a machine with hard limits and an era with firm opinions about what software should weigh.
